In chapter 6 we judged the market from outside the firm. In this chapter we look inside the firm, at what it owns and what it can do.

The resource-based view

Two events companies can face exactly the same five forces and still perform very differently. For example: both organisers bid for the same resort contracts, buy from the same boat operators and sell to the same guests. One of them wins most of the large contracts year after year. It holds long-term agreements with three resorts, and its crew can set up a 500-guest conference overnight. The other organiser holds no such agreements and no such crew.

With the resource-based view (RBV) we explain that difference from inside the firm. Firms in one industry hold different bundles of resources, and some of those resources are hard to move from one firm to another. A firm whose resources are both valuable and hard for rivals to obtain can earn more than its rivals in the same market (Barney, 1991). With Porter’s Five Forces in chapter 6 we judged the market. With the RBV we judge the firm.

We separate two kinds of internal strength:

  • A resource is something the firm owns or controls: a brand, a customer database, cash reserves, a location, a licence. Resources can be tangible, such as equipment, or intangible, such as reputation.
  • A capability is something the firm can do by combining resources through practised routines: planning a 500-guest conference in a week, or recovering a wedding when the boat transfer fails.

A capability usually rests on 2 or more resources at once. A trained crew, a stock of equipment and a booking system together give the capability of fast set-up.

The VRIO framework

We judge whether a resource gives a firm an advantage by asking four questions about it. For example: our events company’s database of 4,000 past guests helps it reach returning customers, and rivals may hold similar records. To judge a resource like this, we ask the four questions in order. Barney (1995) set them out as the VRIO framework, named from the first letter of each question:

We stop at the first “No”, and its position gives the competitive implication:

Competitive parity means the firm stays level with its rivals, because every rival has the resource. A temporary advantage lasts until rivals copy the resource. An unexploited advantage is a resource the firm holds and uses only in part. A sustained competitive advantage needs four Yes answers.

Worked example 7.1

Two resources at Company B

The file codes two of Company B’s resources as follows:

Walk each resource through the VRIO questions, name its competitive implication, and state what management should do with it.

Customer database: valuable, yes. Rare, yes. Costly to imitate, no, so we stop at the third question. The implication is a temporary advantage. The organised answer comes after the stop, so it plays no part. Rivals can build a similar database at modest cost, so Company B should use its lead while it lasts, for example by launching offers to past guests this season.

Skilled workforce: yes to the first three questions, then no to organised. The implication is an unexploited advantage. The company holds a valuable, rare and hard-to-copy workforce, and its structure or incentives leave part of that value unused. The action is internal: give the skilled staff the roles, authority and rewards to use their skills on the company’s main work.

Reading a company’s VRIO profile

From one resource we learn little about a whole firm, so we count the implications across all 8 of a company’s resources and read the counts as a VRIO profile:

Half of the 40 resources, 20, give only competitive parity. That pattern is common: most of what a firm owns, its rivals own too. An advantage comes from the few resources that pass more of the questions.

Worked example 7.2

Which company can carry a differentiation strategy?

Companies B and C both want to follow a differentiation strategy (chapter 6): an offer that customers value and cannot get from rivals.

Use Table 7.4 to compare the two companies, and decide which one has the internal strength to support that strategy.

Company B: 4 sustained advantages, from its brand reputation, location network, supplier relationships and technology platform. It also holds 2 temporary advantages and 1 unexploited advantage, and only 1 resource gives parity.

Company C: 1 sustained advantage, its skilled workforce. 5 resources give parity and 2 put it at a disadvantage.

Decision: Company B has the broader support for differentiation. Its brand, locations, suppliers and technology are hard for rivals to match, so managers can examine an offer built on those four resources. Company C has one sustained advantage, its workforce, and its profile gives little evidence about how that workforce combines with its other resources. Before choosing differentiation, Company C’s managers would assess whether the whole bundle can deliver a distinctive offer.

Dynamic capabilities

A VRIO rating applies at one point in time. Markets change, and a resource rated Yes on all four questions this year can earn a No on one of them later. For example: in chapter 6 a virtual conference was a substitute for an in-person event. If more companies hold their meetings online, our events company’s resort contracts become less valuable, however hard they are to copy.

Dynamic capabilities are a firm’s abilities to change its own resources as the market moves (Teece et al., 1997). Teece (2007) groups them into three:

When rivals copy a temporary advantage, a firm with strong dynamic capabilities can build new advantages to replace it.

Using VRIO in a recommendation

A recommendation uses VRIO in the same three steps as Porter in chapter 6: the evidence, the framework, then the action. We also use VRIO to rate feasibility on the Strategic Options Matrix, because the feasibility of an option depends on whether the firm holds the resources the option needs.

Worked example 7.3

Is the international conference option feasible? (illustrative)

In chapter 6, international conferences were our events company’s most attractive option, rated 8, with a feasibility of only 3. A planning team codes the three resources the option needs, in an illustrative coding.

Classify each resource, and recommend what the company should do before pursuing the option.

Classification: - The resort contracts give a sustained competitive advantage: yes to all four. - The event crew gives competitive parity, since every rival has a crew. - The overseas client network gives an unexploited advantage: the contacts exist through two senior staff, but the company has no sales role or system to turn them into bookings.

Recommendation: the company holds the venue side of the option already. The gap is the organised question for the client network. We recommend creating an international sales role that works the overseas contacts, then re-rating the option’s feasibility after one season of enquiries. The parity rating places the crew level with rivals, so the company would still check the crew’s capacity and skills for large international events. In this illustrative coding the advantage comes from the venue contracts and, once organised, the client network.

TipTry It 7.6: Two options, both frameworks

Our events company must choose one market for the next twelve months, in an illustrative scenario. Option A is wedding receptions in the capital’s hotels: demand is the largest in the country and bookings come all year. Option B is dining events on a sandbank near the capital: guests pay premium prices, and weather can cancel a night.

For Option A, the company’s main resource is its stock of wedding décor, which every planner in the capital also holds. For Option B, it holds the only 10-year permit for the sandbank, which took three years to obtain, and a marine-safety crew trained to run events there.

  1. Calculate the mean force score and the attractiveness of each option.
  2. Classify the main resource for each option with VRIO.
  3. Recommend one option, and name one risk of your choice.
NoteCheck your answer

1. Attractiveness. Option A: (8 + 6 + 7 + 6 + 4) ÷ 5 = 31 ÷ 5 = 6.2, so attractiveness is 10 − 6.2 = 3.8, rounded to 4. Option B: (3 + 2 + 5 + 6 + 5) ÷ 5 = 21 ÷ 5 = 4.2, so attractiveness is 10 − 4.2 = 5.8, rounded to 6. Option A’s strongest pressures are rivalry (8) and the hotels as suppliers (7).

2. VRIO. The décor stock is valuable and held by every rival, so it gives competitive parity. The sandbank permit is valuable, rare and costly to imitate, and the trained crew organises it, so it gives a sustained competitive advantage.

3. Recommendation. We recommend Option B: it is the more attractive market, 6 against 4, and the company holds a resource there that rivals cannot copy. In Option A the company would compete on equal terms against many planners, with the hotels setting the price of the venue. The main risk of Option B is weather: cancelled nights cost revenue and reputation, so the company needs an indoor fallback venue and clear cancellation terms.

A case for Option A earns credit when it weighs the larger, year-round demand against the rivalry and the parity resource, and names how the company would build an advantage there.

Limitations

  • Every Yes or No is a judgement, so a report gives the evidence behind each one.
  • We apply VRIO to one resource at a time. An advantage often comes from a bundle, such as crew, equipment and systems together.
  • A VRIO profile describes one moment. How long an advantage lasts depends on the firm’s dynamic capabilities.
  • With VRIO we look inside the firm only. A strong resource in an unattractive market still needs the external view from chapter 6.

Section summary

We use the resource-based view to explain differences in performance from inside the firm. Resources are what a firm owns or controls, and capabilities are what it can do by combining them. With the VRIO framework we ask four questions in order: valuable, rare, costly to imitate and organised. The first “No” sets the implication: disadvantage, parity, temporary advantage or unexploited advantage, and four Yes answers mean a sustained competitive advantage. In the resource file, 20 of the 40 resources give parity, and Company B, with 4 sustained advantages, has the strongest profile. Through sensing, seizing and transforming, a firm can renew its advantages as the market changes. We use a VRIO judgement to rate an option’s feasibility, and we build a recommendation from the evidence, the framework and an action.
