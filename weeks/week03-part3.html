Part 3: Project time, the critical path and Gantt charts

Keywords

PERT, three-point estimate, critical path method, CPM, CPA, slack, float, Gantt chart, project scheduling

In parts 1 and 2 we evaluated uncertain outcomes in money. In this part we evaluate them in time: we calculate how long preparation will take, identify the tasks that set the completion date and calculate the probability of meeting a deadline.

NoteWhere we are in this chapter

Our events team is planning a one-day training event and has three questions to answer:

  1. How many of the people who register will attend?
  2. Which of two catering vendors should we book?
  3. How many working days do we need to be ready?

In part 1 we used 100 registration records to find that a registered person attends with probability 0.83. In part 2 we compared two catering vendors using expected value and a risk review, and we used the binomial model to estimate how many people will attend.

In this part we answer the third question, using two files. Week3_CPM_Task_Data.csv lists the 8 tasks we must finish before the event. For each task it gives three estimates of the duration in working days, and the tasks that must finish before it can start. Week3_Gantt_Data.csv holds the same 8 tasks as a schedule, with a start day, an end day and the team responsible for each.

Projects as linked tasks

A project is a set of tasks that together produce one result, such as an event, by a fixed date. Some tasks can start at once. Others must wait for earlier tasks to finish. A task that must finish before another can start is that task’s predecessor.

Two names cover the same method. Critical path analysis (CPA) is the usual name in the UK, and the critical path method (CPM) is the usual name in the United States. In this chapter we use the abbreviation CPM.

The CPM file, Week3_CPM_Task_Data.csv, lists the 8 tasks we must finish before the event. Each row gives three estimates of the task’s duration in working days, explained in Three-point estimates and PERT, and the task’s predecessors. To keep Table 3.18 narrow, the predecessors of Staff_Briefing are given by their IDs: “3, 4, 5 and 6” means tasks 3, 4, 5 and 6.

In Figure 3.4 the tasks are drawn as a network. Each box is a task, and each arrow runs from a predecessor to the task that waits for it. Reading the arrows from left to right gives the order of work. The figure also marks two results you will calculate later in this part: the slack of each task and the critical path.

One link in the network adds nothing. Staff_Briefing lists Catering_Arrangement as a predecessor, but the briefing also waits for AV_and_Equipment_Setup, which itself starts only once catering has finished. So the briefing can never start before catering ends, and the direct link from catering to the briefing is redundant. In the figure it is drawn as a dotted arrow. To find a redundant link, read the network and the table together.

Three-point estimates and PERT

A task duration is uncertain, just as a vendor’s delivery time is. The programme evaluation and review technique (PERT) asks the people who know each task for three estimates:

  • Optimistic, \(O\): the duration if everything goes well
  • Most likely, \(M\): the usual duration
  • Pessimistic, \(P\): the duration if things go badly, short of disaster

PERT combines them into an expected duration, \(T_E\). This is a weighted average: the most likely estimate counts four times as much as each of the other two.

\[T_E = \frac{O + 4M + P}{6}\]

We divide by 6 because the weights add to \(1 + 4 + 1 = 6\). PERT also gives each task a variance, which measures how uncertain its duration is. PERT assumes that the range from the optimistic to the pessimistic estimate covers about six standard deviations, so one standard deviation is one sixth of that range:

\[\sigma = \frac{P - O}{6} \qquad \sigma^2 = \left(\frac{P - O}{6}\right)^2\]

A task with a wide gap between its optimistic and pessimistic estimates has a large variance, and a task whose estimates sit close together has a small variance.

Worked example 3.10

The expected duration of the venue booking

The venue booking takes 2 days at best, 5 days most likely and 6 days at worst.

Calculate its PERT expected duration, standard deviation and variance. Explain why the expected duration falls below the most likely estimate.

Expected duration:

\[T_E = \frac{2 + 4 \times 5 + 6}{6}\] \[= \frac{2 + 20 + 6}{6} = \frac{28}{6} = 4.667 \text{ days}\]

Standard deviation and variance:

\[\sigma = \frac{6 - 2}{6} = 0.667 \text{ days}\] \[\sigma^2 = \left(\frac{4}{6}\right)^2 = \frac{16}{36} = 0.444\]

Why it falls below 5: The optimistic estimate sits 3 days below the most likely one, and the pessimistic estimate only 1 day above it. So the estimates lean towards an early finish, and the average falls below 5. When the pessimistic estimate sits far above the most likely one, the opposite happens and the expected duration is above the most likely estimate.

The forward pass

The forward pass works through the network from left to right and finds the earliest time each task can start and finish. We count time in days from the start of the project, so time 0 is the start of day 1, and time 5 is the end of day 5.

  • Earliest start, ES: a task with no predecessors starts at 0. A task with predecessors starts when the last of them finishes, so its ES is the largest EF among its predecessors.
  • Earliest finish, EF: the earliest start plus the duration.

\[EF = ES + \text{duration}\]

The project’s earliest finishing time is the largest EF of all its tasks.

The backward pass and the critical path

The backward pass works from right to left and finds the latest time each task can start and finish without delaying the project.

  • Latest finish, LF: a task with no successors finishes at the project’s finishing time. A task with successors must finish before the first of them has to start, so its LF is the smallest LS among its successors.
  • Latest start, LS: the latest finish minus the duration.

\[LS = LF - \text{duration}\]

The gap between the two passes is the task’s slack, also called float: the number of days the task can be delayed without moving the project’s finishing date.

\[\text{Slack} = LS - ES = LF - EF\]

A task with zero slack is a critical task. The chain of critical tasks from the start of the project to its end is the critical path. Its total duration is the project’s finishing time, because it is the longest chain of linked tasks in the network. Any delay to a critical task delays the entire project.

Worked example 3.11

The critical path for the event

Use the most likely durations from Table 3.18 and the predecessors shown there.

Run the forward and backward passes, calculate each task’s slack and name the critical path and the project’s finishing day.

Forward pass, left to right:

  • Venue_Booking and Speaker_Confirmation have no predecessors, so each starts at 0. They finish at \(0 + 5 = 5\) and \(0 + 3 = 3\).
  • Marketing_Launch and Catering_Arrangement each wait for the venue: ES 5, EF \(5 + 5 = 10\) and \(5 + 4 = 9\).
  • Registration_System_Setup waits for the speakers: ES 3, EF \(3 + 5 = 8\).
  • AV_and_Equipment_Setup waits for catering: ES 9, EF \(9 + 5 = 14\).
  • Staff_Briefing waits for four tasks, finishing at 10, 8, 9 and 14. It starts at the largest, 14, and finishes at 15.
  • Final_Rehearsal: ES 15 and EF 16, so the project finishes at 16.

Backward pass, right to left:

  • Final_Rehearsal has LF 16 and LS \(16 - 1 = 15\), so Staff_Briefing has LF 15 and LS 14.
  • AV_and_Equipment_Setup, Marketing_Launch and Registration_System_Setup each feed only the briefing, so each has LF 14. Their LS values are \(14 - 5 = 9\), \(14 - 5 = 9\) and \(14 - 5 = 9\).
  • Catering_Arrangement feeds the AV setup (LS 9) and the briefing (LS 14). Take the smaller: LF 9, LS \(9 - 4 = 5\).
  • Venue_Booking feeds marketing (LS 9) and catering (LS 5), so its LF is 5 and its LS is 0.
  • Speaker_Confirmation feeds registration (LS 9), so its LF is 9 and its LS is \(9 - 3 = 6\).

Critical path: Venue_Booking, Catering_Arrangement, AV_and_Equipment_Setup, Staff_Briefing, Final_Rehearsal. Its durations add to \(5 + 4 + 5 + 1 + 1 = 16\) days, the project’s finishing time.

Slack: Marketing can be delayed by up to 4 days. Speaker confirmation and registration setup each have 6 days of slack. The two tasks are on the same chain, so they share those 6 days: if speaker confirmation is delayed by 6 days, registration setup has no slack left.

The same path with PERT durations

Repeating the two passes with the PERT expected durations from Table 3.19 yields the same critical path, with an expected length of:

\[4.667 + 4.000 + 5.000 + 1.167 + 1.167 = \frac{28 + 24 + 30 + 7 + 7}{6}\] \[= \frac{96}{6} = 16.0 \text{ days}\]

The slack shrinks a little for the other tasks: 4.17 days for marketing and 5.83 days for the speaker and registration chain. In The probability of meeting a deadline we use the PERT figures to calculate the probability of meeting a deadline.

The probability of meeting a deadline

With PERT, each task’s duration is uncertain, so the finishing date is a random variable. We can then use the normal model from chapter 2 to estimate the probability of meeting a deadline, in three steps:

  1. Mean of the path: Add the expected durations along the critical path: here, 16.0 days.
  2. Variance of the path: Add the variances of the tasks on the critical path. This assumes the task durations are independent of one another.
  3. Probability: Treat the path’s duration as normal, find the z-score of the deadline and read the area below it from the standard normal table.

\[\sigma^2_{\text{path}} = \sum \sigma^2_{\text{task}} \qquad z = \frac{\text{deadline} - T_{\text{path}}}{\sigma_{\text{path}}}\]

where \(T_{\text{path}}\) is the expected length of the critical path and \(\sigma_{\text{path}}\) is the square root of its variance.

Variances add along a path, so to get the standard deviation of the path, add the variances first and then take the square root of the total.

Worked example 3.12

Ready in 18 working days?

The venue manager asks us to confirm that the event will be ready within 18 working days of the start of planning.

Calculate the variance and standard deviation of the critical path from Table 3.19, and estimate the probability of finishing within 18 days. State the limits of the estimate.

Variance of the path: Add the five critical tasks’ variances. Each is a whole number of 36ths, because it is a difference divided by 6 and then squared: 0.444 is \(16/36\) and 0.028 is \(1/36\). Adding the fractions avoids rounding error.

\[\sigma^2_{\text{path}} = \frac{16 + 16 + 16 + 1 + 1}{36} = \frac{50}{36} = 1.389\]

Standard deviation: \(\sigma_{\text{path}} = \sqrt{1.389} = 1.18\) days.

z-score of the deadline:

\[z = \frac{18 - 16.0}{1.18} = \frac{2.0}{1.18} = 1.70\]

The standard normal table gives an area of 0.9554 below 1.70.

Answer: The event has a probability of about 0.96 of being ready within 18 working days. If we promise 18 days, we will miss the deadline about once in 22 projects.

Limits: The estimate assumes the five task durations are independent. A single cause, such as a staff shortage, can delay three tasks simultaneously. It assumes the duration of a five-task path follows a normal distribution, which is an approximation. It also ignores the other paths. The next-longest path, through marketing, is expected to take 11.8 days. It is therefore highly unlikely to exceed the duration of the critical path. In a project where two paths have similar durations, the true risk is higher than this method calculates.

TipTry It 3.10: Choosing a promise

Use the critical path figures from worked example 3.12: a mean of 16.0 days and a standard deviation of 1.18 days.

  1. Estimate the probability of finishing within 17 days.
  2. Estimate the probability of finishing within 15 days.
  3. The venue manager wants a date we will meet 95 times in 100. Which day should we promise?
NoteCheck your answer
  1. \(z = (17 - 16.0) \div 1.18 = 0.85\). The area below 0.85 is 0.8023, about 80 per cent.
  2. \(z = (15 - 16.0) \div 1.18 = -0.85\). The area below \(-0.85\) is \(1 - 0.8023 = 0.1977\), about 20 per cent.
  3. The 95th percentile has \(z = 1.645\), which gives \(16.0 + 1.645 \times 1.18 = 16.0 + 1.94 = 17.94\) days, so promise 18 working days.

Gantt charts

A Gantt chart is a project schedule drawn as horizontal bars against a calendar. Each task has its own row, and its bar runs from its start day to its finish day. You can see at a glance which tasks run at the same time, who is responsible for each and when the project ends. From the network diagram we read the order of the tasks, and from the Gantt chart we read their dates.

The Gantt file, Week3_Gantt_Data.csv, holds our schedule. It gives each task a start day, a duration, an end day, the team responsible and a flag for the critical path. The schedule is drawn in Figure 3.5.

Day numbers and CPM times

In the Gantt file, days are numbered from 1. In CPM, time is counted from 0. A task with ES 0 and EF 5 occupies days 1 to 5. Two rules convert one to the other:

\[\text{Start day} = ES + 1 \qquad \text{End day} = EF\]

For example: catering has ES 5 and EF 9 in Table 3.20, so it runs from day 6 to day 9.

Reading and checking a Gantt chart

A Gantt chart may appear complete once the bars are drawn. Before you use it to manage the project, check it against the network with the five checks in Table 3.21, which cover most errors.

In the slack check we find a limitation of the chart. A Gantt chart drawn from earliest starts shows when work can begin and leaves out how late each task could begin. Some teams draw the slack as a thin line after each bar. Others state it beside the chart.

Worked example 3.13

Moving a task inside its slack

The marketing team is occupied until day 8 and asks to start the marketing launch on day 9, three days after its planned start on day 6.

Using Table 3.20 and Figure 3.5, decide whether the request delays the event, and state how the Gantt chart changes.

The slack: Marketing_Launch has ES 5 and LS 9, so it can start as late as time 9, which is day 10, with no effect on the finishing date. Day 9 is time 8, which uses 3 of the 4 days of slack.

The new dates: Starting on day 9, the 5-day task runs to day 13. The staff briefing starts on day 15, so marketing still ends a day before it is needed.

Decision: Approve the request, because the event will still finish on day 16.

The chart: Move the marketing bar to days 9 to 13. Marketing now has 1 day of slack remaining. Note on the chart that a further delay of more than 1 day will delay the briefing.

Section summary

A project is a network of tasks linked by predecessors. PERT turns three estimates into an expected duration, \((O + 4M + P)/6\), and a variance, \(((P - O)/6)^2\). The forward pass finds each task’s earliest start and finish, taking the largest finish among its predecessors. The backward pass finds the latest start and finish, taking the smallest start among its successors. Slack is the gap between the two, and the tasks with zero slack form the critical path, which sets the finishing date: 16 days for our event. Adding the variances along the critical path and applying chapter 2’s normal model gives the probability of meeting a deadline. A Gantt chart is the schedule drawn on a calendar, and you need to check it against the network before you rely on it.
