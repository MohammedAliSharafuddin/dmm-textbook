Part 1: Probability from records

Keywords

probability, complement rule, contingency table, joint probability, conditional probability, addition rule, probability tree, decision making under uncertainty

NoteThe decision question for this chapter

An events team is planning a one-day training event and brings you three questions. How many of the people who register will turn up? Which of two catering vendors should the team book? And how many working days does the team need to be ready, and which tasks can never be allowed to slip?

Chapters 1 and 2 described what had already happened. This chapter looks forward. Each of the three questions has an answer that is uncertain when the decision is made, and each needs a different tool: probability for attendance, expected value and a decision tree for the vendor, and the critical path method with a Gantt chart for the schedule.

You will use four small files, one for each tool: Week3_Probability_Basics_Data.csv (100 registration records), Week3_Decision_Tree_Data.csv (50 vendor scenarios, two options each), Week3_CPM_Task_Data.csv (8 project tasks with three time estimates) and Week3_Gantt_Data.csv (the same 8 tasks as a schedule). Every figure quoted in this chapter comes from those files. The dataset overview lists them with the other files.

Decisions under uncertainty

A decision under uncertainty is a choice made before you know how it will turn out. Booking a venue for 120 people is a decision under uncertainty, because some of the 120 will stay at home. Choosing a vendor is one, because a vendor can deliver late. Promising a date for an event is one, because tasks overrun.

Uncertainty requires structured reasoning. Records of past events show how often people fail to turn up. Past contracts show how often each vendor delivered on time. Previous task managers can estimate typical durations and worst-case delays. A manager who quantifies this evidence can compare options objectively and justify the choice to stakeholders.

Every decision tool in this chapter follows the same eight steps.

Steps 1 to 3 need judgement and no arithmetic. Steps 4 to 6 are the arithmetic this chapter teaches. Steps 7 and 8 return to judgement, and they decide whether the arithmetic is used well.

Five terms for this chapter

Five terms appear throughout the chapter, and each gets a full section later. You need to recognise all five now, as the following pages use them before their defining sections appear.

A probability is a number from 0 to 1 that states how likely an event is. A probability of 0.83 means the event happens in about 83 cases out of 100.

An event is an outcome you can count in the records: a registered person attended, a vendor delivered late, a task finished on day 16.

An expected value is the average outcome of an uncertain option, with each possible outcome weighted by its probability. Part 2 shows how to calculate one.

A decision tree is a diagram that sets out the options, the uncertain events that follow each option and the outcome at the end of every path.

The critical path is the longest chain of linked tasks in a project. It sets the earliest finishing date, and any delay to a task on it delays the whole project. Part 3 shows how to find it.

Probability from records

A probability from records is the share of past cases in which an event happened. You count the cases where the event happened and divide by the number of cases the question is about.

\[P(A) = \frac{\text{number of cases in which } A \text{ happened}}{\text{number of cases the question is about}}\]

\(P(A)\) is read as “the probability of \(A\)”. Three properties follow from the definition:

  • Every probability lies between 0 and 1. A probability of 0 means the event never happened in the records, and 1 means it happened every time.
  • The probabilities of all the possible outcomes of one variable add to 1. A registered person either attended or did not, so the two probabilities cover every case.
  • A probability multiplied by 100 is a percentage. A probability of 0.83 and a share of 83 per cent state the same fact.

The probability file holds 100 registration records from past events, one row per person who registered. Each row records whether the person attended, the type of ticket and how it was paid for.

Where a probability comes from

A probability from records is one of three kinds a manager meets.

  • From records: 83 of 100 registered people attended, so the probability of attending is 0.83. This chapter uses records wherever they exist.
  • From judgement: an experienced events manager estimates that a new venue has a 0.9 chance of being ready on time. Judgement fills the gap when no records exist, and the report names it as judgement.
  • From equally likely outcomes: a fair die has six faces, so each has a probability of \(1/6\). Games of chance work this way. Business events rarely do.

A probability from records describes the past. It serves as a forecast when the coming event resembles the past ones: a similar audience, a similar price and a similar reminder process. When those conditions change, the probability needs fresh evidence.

A probability as a long-run share

A probability from records rests on one idea: over many repetitions, the share of cases in which an event happens settles towards its probability. A short run can stray a long way from it. The coin toss below shows the idea on a fair coin, whose probability of heads is 0.5.

If the panel above stays blank, open the probability explorer in a new tab.

  1. Press Toss 10 three times. Each run gives a different share of heads, and some land well away from 0.50.
  2. Press Reset, then Toss 10,000. The chart shows the share wandering over the first few hundred tosses and then settling close to 0.50.

The 100 registration records work the same way. 83 attendances in 100 give a probability of 0.83, and a file of 10 records could easily have given 0.7 or 0.9. The more records behind a probability, the closer it settles to the rate the next event will show.

The complement rule

The complement of an event is everything outside it. The complement of “attended” is “did not attend”, which the file records as No_Show. An event and its complement cover every case between them, so their probabilities add to 1.

\[P(\text{not } A) = 1 - P(A)\]

The complement rule simplifies calculation. When an event is hard to count and its complement is easy, count the complement and subtract from 1.

Worked example 3.1

Attendance and its complement

The probability file holds 100 registration records, and 83 of them record Attended.

Calculate the probability that a registered person attends, then use the complement rule to find the probability of a no-show. State what the two figures mean for a team expecting 100 registrations.

Attendance: The question is about all registered people, so the denominator is all 100 records.

\[P(\text{Attended}) = \frac{83}{100} = 0.83\]

No-show, by the complement rule:

\[P(\text{No\_Show}) = 1 - 0.83 = 0.17\]

The file confirms it: 17 records read No_Show, and \(17 \div 100 = 0.17\).

Meaning: If the next event resembles the past ones, about 83 of every 100 registered people will attend and about 17 seats will stay empty. A team ordering food for every registration will pay for approximately 17 uneaten meals per 100 registrations. Part 2 shows how far the real number can stray from 83.

  2. Use the complement rule to find the probability of a no-show.
  3. The next course has 160 registrations. About how many people should the centre expect to attend, if the next course resembles the last?

Contingency tables and joint probability

A contingency table counts the cases that fall into each combination of two categorical variables. Each row is one category of the first variable, each column is one category of the second and each cell counts the cases that belong to both. Chapter 1 called the same layout a cross-tabulation.

Table 3.3 crosses ticket type with attendance for the 100 registration records.

Each number in the table answers a different question.

  • A cell counts the cases in two categories at once. 16 people held a VIP ticket and attended.
  • A row total or a column total counts one category on its own. 20 people held a VIP ticket. These totals sit in the margins of the table, so a probability calculated from one is called a marginal probability.
  • The grand total, 100, counts every case.

A joint probability is the probability that two events happen together. Divide a cell by the grand total:

\[P(A \text{ and } B) = \frac{\text{number of cases in both } A \text{ and } B}{\text{number of cases the question is about}}\]

For example: the probability that a registered person held a VIP ticket and attended is \(16 \div 100 = 0.16\). The marginal probability of a VIP ticket is \(20 \div 100 = 0.20\).

A joint probability is never larger than either of its marginal probabilities, because every case counted in the cell is also counted in its row and in its column. If a joint probability exceeds a marginal one, a counting error has occurred.

Worked example 3.2

Reading a contingency table

The events team crosses payment method with attendance.

Calculate the probability that a registered person paid in cash, the probability that a registered person paid in cash and did not attend, and the probability that a registered person paid by bank transfer and attended. Name which of the three is a marginal probability.

Paid in cash: Read the row total. 30 of the 100 people paid in cash.

\[P(\text{Cash}) = \frac{30}{100} = 0.30\]

This is the marginal probability, taken from the margin of the table.

Paid in cash and did not attend: Read the cell where the Cash row meets the No_Show column.

\[P(\text{Cash and No\_Show}) = \frac{7}{100} = 0.07\]

Paid by bank transfer and attended:

\[P(\text{Bank\_Transfer and Attended}) = \frac{34}{100} = 0.34\]

Both joint probabilities use all 100 records as the denominator, because the question is about every registered person. Seven people in every 100 registrations are cash payers who stay away.

Conditional probability

A conditional probability is the probability of one event among the cases where a second event has happened. The second event narrows the denominator to a subgroup. The word “given” signals it, and so do the phrases “among”, “of those who” and “within”.

\[P(A \mid B) = \frac{\text{number of cases in both } A \text{ and } B}{\text{number of cases in } B} = \frac{P(A \text{ and } B)}{P(B)}\]

\(P(A \mid B)\) is read as “the probability of \(A\) given \(B\)”. The vertical bar stands for “given”.

For example: among the 20 VIP ticket holders, 16 attended.

\[P(\text{Attended} \mid \text{VIP}) = \frac{16}{20} = 0.80\]

The same figure comes from the second form of the formula: \(0.16 \div 0.20 = 0.80\).

The denominator decides the question

The three kinds of probability differ only in their denominator. Choose the denominator from the wording of the question, and the arithmetic follows.

The last two rows reverse the condition. \(P(\text{Attended} \mid \text{VIP})\) asks about VIP ticket holders. \(P(\text{VIP} \mid \text{No\_Show})\) asks about the people who stayed away. Both use the cell of 16 or the cell of 4, and they answer different questions, so they give different numbers. Read the word after “given” or “among” and use its count as the denominator.

Worked example 3.3

Which group needs a reminder?

The events team can afford to send a reminder message to one payment group before the next event. Use Table 3.4.

Calculate the probability of attending within each payment method, decide which group should receive the reminder, and explain why the joint probabilities give the wrong comparison for this decision.

Attendance within each payment method: The question is “among the people who paid this way, what share attended?”, so each row total is the denominator.

Decision: Send the reminder to cash payers. They attend at 0.767, the lowest rate of the three and 6 points below the overall rate of 0.830.

Why the joint probabilities mislead here: The joint probabilities of attending, 0.34 for bank transfer, 0.23 for cash and 0.26 for credit card, mostly reflect how many people used each method. Bank transfer has the most payers, so it has the most attenders. Divide by each group’s own size and the comparison becomes fair.

One caution applies here: with only 30 cash payers, a gap of 6 points is about 2 people. Chapter 10 gives a test that decides whether a gap of this size is larger than chance. For a cheap reminder message, the evidence is enough to act on.

The addition rule

The addition rule gives the probability that at least one of two events happens: \(A\), or \(B\), or both. Add the two probabilities, then subtract the joint probability, because the cases in both events were counted once in each.

\[P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) - P(A \text{ and } B)\]

For example: a registered person who holds a VIP ticket or paid in cash. From the file, 20 people hold a VIP ticket and 30 paid in cash. Five people did both, so adding 20 and 30 counts those 5 twice.

\[P(\text{VIP or Cash}) = 0.20 + 0.30 - 0.05 = 0.45\]

A count confirms it: \(20 + 30 - 5 = 45\) different people, out of 100.

Mutually exclusive events

Two events are mutually exclusive when no case can belong to both. Each registration record carries exactly one payment method, so “paid in cash” and “paid by credit card” are mutually exclusive. Their joint probability is 0, and the addition rule loses its last term:

\[P(A \text{ or } B) = P(A) + P(B) \quad \text{when } A \text{ and } B \text{ are mutually exclusive}\]

\[P(\text{Cash or Credit\_Card}) = 0.30 + 0.30 = 0.60\]

An event and its complement are always mutually exclusive, and together they cover every case. The complement rule is the addition rule applied to that pair: \(P(A) + P(\text{not } A) = 1\).

Worked example 3.4

Sizing a follow-up list

The events team wants to telephone every registered person who either paid in cash or holds a VIP ticket. Table 3.7 crosses the two variables.

Calculate the share of registered people on the call list, and the number of calls to expect for an event with 200 registrations.

Addition rule: The events overlap, since 5 VIP ticket holders paid in cash.

\[P(\text{VIP or Cash}) = P(\text{VIP}) + P(\text{Cash}) - P(\text{VIP and Cash})\] \[= \frac{20}{100} + \frac{30}{100} - \frac{5}{100} = 0.20 + 0.30 - 0.05 = 0.45\]

Check by counting cells: The VIP row holds \(9 + 5 + 6 = 20\) people. The Cash column adds the 25 Standard cash payers who are outside the VIP row. \(20 + 25 = 45\).

Calls for 200 registrations: \(0.45 \times 200 = 90\) calls. Adding the two probabilities without subtracting the overlap would yield 0.50 and 100 calls, incorrectly budgeting for 10 individuals.

Independence and the multiplication rule

Two events are independent when knowing that one has happened leaves the probability of the other unchanged. In symbols, \(A\) and \(B\) are independent when

\[P(A \mid B) = P(A)\]

Independence is an empirical claim tested by the records. Compare the conditional probability with the overall one.

  • Ticket type and attendance: \(P(\text{Attended}) = 0.83\). Among VIP ticket holders it is 0.80, and among Standard ticket holders it is \(67 \div 80 = 0.8375\). The three figures are close, so ticket type tells you little about whether a person attends. The two variables are close to independent.
  • Payment method and attendance: Among cash payers the rate falls to 0.767, against 0.83 overall. Knowing that a person paid in cash reduces the probability of attendance. Worked example 3.3 used exactly that difference.

A sample of 100 records rarely gives two exactly equal figures, even when two variables are independent in the wider population. Chapter 10’s chi-squared test decides whether a difference is larger than sampling variation. In this chapter, judge it by size and by what it would change.

The multiplication rule

The multiplication rule turns the conditional probability formula round to give a joint probability:

\[P(A \text{ and } B) = P(B) \times P(A \mid B)\]

For example: 20 per cent of registered people hold a VIP ticket, and 80 per cent of those attend. \(P(\text{VIP and Attended}) = 0.20 \times 0.80 = 0.16\), the cell value from Table 3.3.

When \(A\) and \(B\) are independent, \(P(A \mid B)\) equals \(P(A)\), and the rule becomes

\[P(A \text{ and } B) = P(A) \times P(B) \quad \text{when } A \text{ and } B \text{ are independent}\]

This shorter form needs independence. Used on dependent events, it gives the wrong joint probability. For cash payers who stay away, the shortcut gives \(0.30 \times 0.17 = 0.051\), about 5 people in 100, where the records hold 7.

Probability trees

A probability tree draws two or more uncertain events in the order they happen. Each branch carries a probability. The branches at the first split carry marginal probabilities, and every later branch carries a probability conditional on the path that leads to it.

Figure 3.1 draws the registration records as a tree. The first split is ticket type, the second is attendance.

Three rules read every probability tree:

  1. Multiply along a path. The probability of a complete path is the product of the probabilities on its branches. This is the multiplication rule: \(P(\text{Standard and Attended}) = 0.80 \times 0.8375 = 0.67\).
  2. Add across paths. The probability of an outcome that two or more paths share is the sum of those paths. Two paths end in attendance, so \(P(\text{Attended}) = 0.67 + 0.16 = 0.83\), the figure from worked example 3.1.
  3. Check the totals. The branches leaving any one point add to 1, and so do the path probabilities: \(0.67 + 0.13 + 0.16 + 0.04 = 1.00\).

A tree is most useful when the stages happen in sequence, and when the second stage depends on the first. Part 2 adds a second kind of point, where the manager chooses the branch, and the probability tree becomes a decision tree.

Worked example 3.5

A tree for a planned event

The events team plans to sell 150 tickets for its next event: 75 per cent Standard and 25 per cent VIP, a higher VIP share than in the past. Assume each ticket type keeps its past attendance rate from Figure 3.1.

Draw the tree as a table of paths, calculate the overall probability of attending, and work out how many attendees to expect.

Paths: The first split uses the new ticket mix. The second keeps the attendance rate given each ticket type.

Overall attendance: Add the two paths that end in attendance.

\[P(\text{Attended}) = 0.6281 + 0.2000 = 0.8281\]

Expected attendees: \(0.8281 \times 150 = 124.2\), so plan for about 124 people.

The new mix barely moves the attendance rate, from 0.830 to 0.828, because the two ticket types attend at almost the same rate. The tree illustrates the reason: a change in the first split matters only when the subsequent branches differ.

Section summary

A decision under uncertainty follows eight steps, from the question to the recommendation, and probability supplies the numbers for steps 4 to 6. A probability from records is a count divided by the number of cases the question is about, and the complement rule gives the chance that an event does not happen. A contingency table holds joint counts in its cells and marginal counts in its margins. A conditional probability narrows the denominator to a subgroup, and reversing the condition changes the question. The addition rule subtracts the overlap that would otherwise be counted twice. Independent events leave each other’s probabilities unchanged, and the multiplication rule joins events along the paths of a probability tree.
