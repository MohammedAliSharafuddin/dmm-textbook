Chapter 8: Ethical Decision Making

NoteThe decision question for this chapter

The events company finds that VIP ticket holders stay away from events more often than other customers. A manager proposes selling 20% more VIP seats than the room holds. Should we act on the result in this way, and if not, what should we do? Three responses are on the table, and the people each one affects have a stake in the choice.

This chapter uses DMM_Teaching_Data_Full.csv, the same 200 customer records as chapters 1 to 5 and 9. Every figure attributed to that file comes from it. The stakeholder ratings and the matrix scores are illustrations, and each is marked as such.

In chapters 6 and 7 we judged the market and the firm. In this chapter we judge the option itself: who it affects, and whether it treats them fairly.

The no-show result

The no-show result concerns customers who registered for an event and stayed away. Our teaching file contains each customer’s ticket type and attendance status:

VIP ticket holders stayed away most often, 7 of 30. With the chi-squared test from chapter 10 we obtain \(p = .048\) for this pattern, just below .05: if ticket type made no difference to attendance, a pattern at least this uneven would arise by chance about 5 times in 100. A manager reads the result and proposes overbooking VIP sessions by 20%: selling 36 VIP tickets for a room of 30 seats, on the expectation that about a quarter of the buyers will stay away.

Five features of the evidence matter before anyone acts on it:

  • Group size: the VIP rate rests on 30 customers and 7 absences. If 2 of the 7 had attended, the rate would be 5 of 30, or 16.7%, level with Standard.
  • Distance from .05: the p-value of .048 sits just below the line, so a slightly different sample could fall on the other side of it.
  • A small expected count: if ticket type made no difference, we would expect 4.5 VIP holders to stay away. Counts that small make the p-value less dependable, and we check them with the rule in chapter 10.
  • Where the unevenness lies: Student holders account for most of it. At the overall rate of 15%, about 6.9 of the 46 Student holders would have stayed away, and 2 did. About 4.5 of the 30 VIP holders would have stayed away, and 7 did. The Student gap is the larger of the two.
  • The cause: we know VIP holders stayed away more often, and their reasons for staying away are unrecorded. A late transfer, a clash with a resort dinner or a cheaper alternative would each call for a different response.

Worked example 8.1

What overbooking risks

Suppose each of 36 VIP ticket holders attends independently with probability 0.767, the share of VIP holders who attended in Table 8.1. The room holds 30 guests.

Use the binomial model from chapter 3 (counting successes) to calculate the expected attendance and the probability that more than 30 VIP holders arrive. Then state what the overbooking risks.

Expected attendance: \(\mu = n \, p = 36 \times 0.767 = 27.6\), where \(\mu\) is the expected number attending, \(n\) is the number of tickets sold and \(p\) is the probability that one holder attends.

More than 30 arrive: the probability of 31 or more is

=1 - BINOM.DIST(30, 36, 0.767, TRUE), which gives 0.124.

Reading: the model gives an average attendance of 27.6, leaving about 2.4 of the 30 seats empty. In about 1 VIP session in 8, at least one guest who paid the highest price arrives to find no seat. The plan also rests on the 0.767 from 30 customers, the small group in Table 8.1. The probability is a cost to name in the decision, carried by the customers who paid the most.

Stakeholders

A stakeholder is any person or group the decision affects, or who can affect the decision. For the overbooking plan, the stakeholders run well beyond the board that will approve it: the VIP holders who might lose a seat, the staff at the door who would turn them away, the venue partner and a consumer protection regulator.

We place each stakeholder group on a power and interest grid. The grid is widely used and related to Mendelow’s stakeholder analysis, although Mendelow (1981) used power and environmental dynamism as its two axes. Power is how far the group can change the decision or its outcome. Interest is how much the decision affects the group, or how much it cares. Both are rated from 0 to 10, and lines at 5 divide the grid into four quadrants:

The ethical questions often sit in the keep-informed quadrant: groups with a great deal to lose and little say in the room.

Four ethical lenses

The same option can look right from one angle and wrong from another. For example: overbooking fills more VIP seats and raises revenue, which looks good across the company as a whole. It also breaks a promise to the guest who arrives to find no seat. Each of those judgements comes from a different ethical lens, a standard question from moral philosophy about an action. The Markkula Center for Applied Ethics (2021) sets out 6 lenses, and we use 4 of them:

The Markkula Center identifies two further lenses, the common good and virtue, which we can add when a case calls for them.

TipTry It 8.2: Which lens?

Name the lens behind each concern about the overbooking plan.

  1. “Across all our guests, the extra revenue outweighs a few complaints.”
  2. “We sold that guest a seat. Taking it away breaks the contract.”
  3. “Why should VIP buyers carry this risk when Standard buyers also stay away?”
  4. “The guest turned away might have flown in for a family celebration.”
NoteCheck your answer
  1. Utility: it weighs total benefit against total harm.
  2. Rights: it concerns what one person is owed.
  3. Justice: it asks whether a difference in treatment between groups has a relevant reason.
  4. Care: it attends to one person’s actual circumstances.

The ethical decision matrix

An ethical decision matrix scores each option against the statistical evidence and the ethical lenses, with a weight on each criterion. With it we put the trade-off on one page, so the people who disagree can see where they differ. The planning team scores three options from 1 (poor) to 5 (strong). The scores and weights are illustrative, and they match the sample case in the FlairMI tool:

Each option’s weighted total is

\[T = \frac{\sum w_k \, s_k}{\sum w_k}\]

where \(T\) is the option’s weighted total, \(w_k\) is the weight of criterion \(k\), \(s_k\) is the option’s score on criterion \(k\), and \(\sum\) adds over all the criteria. The total stays on the 1 to 5 scale of the scores.

Worked example 8.2

Weighting the three options

Use the scores and weights in Table 8.5.

Calculate the weighted total for each option, rank the options, and compare the ranking with the ranking on statistical evidence alone.

The weights add to \(15 + 20 + 25 + 25 + 15 = 100\).

Overbook VIP sessions: the weighted scores are \(15 \times 5 = 75\), \(20 \times 4 = 80\), \(25 \times 1 = 25\), \(25 \times 2 = 50\) and \(15 \times 1 = 15\). They add to 245, and \(245 \div 100 = 2.45\).

Deposit for VIP buyers only: the weighted scores are 60, 60, 75, 50 and 30. They add to 275, and \(275 \div 100 = 2.75\).

Reminders and free transfers for all: the weighted scores are 30, 60, 125, 125 and 60. They add to 400, and \(400 \div 100 = 4.00\).

Ranking: reminders and free transfers first, at 4.00, then the deposit at 2.75 and overbooking last at 2.45. On the planning team’s illustrative evidence scores alone, the order reverses: overbooking scores 5 and reminders 2. Those scores express how closely the team links the no-show result to each option. The dataset covers ticket type and attendance only, so comparing the three responses themselves would need a trial.

Ethics in reporting the evidence

Ethical judgement also applies when we report a result. When we read a cause into a difference, we can steer a decision toward the wrong people.

Worked example 8.3

A spending gap and the ticket mix

The 30 customers who registered and stayed away have a mean estimated spending of 604.57 USD. The 170 who attended have a mean of 488.19 USD. A draft report says: “Customers who skip our events are our biggest spenders, so marketing should target them.”

From the counts in Table 8.1, 7 of the 30 registered-only customers (23.3%) hold VIP tickets and 2 (6.7%) hold Student tickets, against 23 of 170 (13.5%) and 44 of 170 (25.9%) of attendees. Re-weighted to the attendees’ ticket mix, the registered-only mean is 485.37 USD.

Decide whether the draft’s claim is fair to the evidence, and rewrite it.

The gap: \(604.57 - 488.19 = 116.38\) USD.

The explanation: mean spending differs sharply by ticket type, and the registered-only group holds more VIP tickets and fewer Student tickets. With the same ticket mix as the attendees, the registered-only mean falls to 485.37 USD, against 488.19 USD: a gap of 2.82 USD.

Fairness: the draft attributes the gap to the customers’ behaviour, when re-weighting for ticket mix removes 97.6% of it. A campaign built on the claim would spend money on the wrong explanation.

Rewritten: “Registered-only customers have a higher mean spending, 604.57 USD against 488.19 USD, because more of them hold VIP tickets. With the same ticket mix, the gap falls to 2.82 USD.”

Using ethics in a recommendation

A recommendation that weighs ethics follows the same three steps as chapters 6 and 7, with the ethical reasoning made explicit:

  1. Evidence: the finding, and how strong it is.
  2. Framework: the stakeholders affected and the lenses that weigh against or for the option.
  3. Action: what management should do, and what to monitor.
TipTry It 8.4: Write the recommendation

Write a three-sentence recommendation to the board on the no-show result, with one sentence each for the evidence, the framework and the action. Use worked examples 8.1 and 8.2.

NoteCheck your answer

A model answer: Evidence: VIP ticket holders stayed away more often than other customers, 7 of 30, a result just below the .05 line in a small group, and overbooking a 30-seat VIP session to 36 tickets would turn away at least one paying guest in about 1 session in 8. Framework: the VIP holders and front-of-house staff carry that risk with little say in the decision, and the option scores poorly on rights, justice and care, ranking last of three at 2.45. Action: we recommend reminders and free transfers for all ticket holders, and recording each absent customer’s reason at the next three events, so a later decision rests on a cause.

Your action may differ, so check that every number comes from worked examples 8.1 and 8.2, and that the stakeholders carrying the risk are named.

Limitations

  • Every power, interest, score and weight is a judgement, so a report gives the reason behind each one.
  • The four lenses can point in different directions. We record the trade-off in the matrix, and we resolve the trade-off through the weights we choose.
  • A stakeholder map drawn by the board can leave out the groups with least power. A useful check is to ask who would be surprised by the decision.
  • The binomial calculation in worked example 8.1 assumes each guest decides independently. Guests who travel together may arrive or stay away together, which can make a full room more likely than the binomial calculation suggests.

Section summary

Acting on a statistically strong result can still produce a poor decision. Before acting, we judge the evidence: its size, its group sizes, how close its p-value sits to .05, and whether it bears on cause at all. We map the stakeholders on a power and interest grid, where the groups with high interest and low power often carry the risk. We use the lenses of utility, rights, justice and care to name the ethical concerns, and weight them alongside the evidence in an ethical decision matrix, with the weighted total \(T = \sum w_k s_k / \sum w_k\). In the illustrative no-show matrix, the planning team gives overbooking the highest evidence score, and it ranks last once ethics carry weight. In fair reporting we state the explanation the data support, as the ticket mix does for the spending gap.
